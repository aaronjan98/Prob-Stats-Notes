## Moment Generating Functions

- Recall: If $X_{1}\dots X_{n}$ are iid from a distribution with mgf $M_{X}(t)$
	- Set $Y = X_{1} + \cdots + X_{n}$
		- and $\overline{ X } = \dfrac{X_{1} + \cdots + X_{n}}{n}$
	- Then:
		1. $M_{Y}(t) = \left( M_{X}(t) \right)^n$
		2. $M_{\overline{ X }}(t) = \left( M_{X}\left( \dfrac{t}{n} \right) \right)^n.$
		\* Moment generating function uniquely determines the distribution of a random variable.
		- mgfs are used to prove what kind of random variables you're dealing with

- **Example.** Suppose:
	- $X_{1}\dots X_{n}$ are iid from exponential with scale $\theta > 0.$
	1. Set $Y = X_{1} + \cdots + X_{n}$
		- Find the mgf of $Y.$
			- recall the mgf of exponential: $X \sim \exp(\theta) \Rightarrow M_{X}(t) = \dfrac{1}{1-\theta t}$
		- $\Rightarrow M_{Y}(t) = \left( \dfrac{1}{1 - \theta} \right)^n$
			- this is the mgf of the gamma distribution
	2. Based on the mgf of $Y,$ what is the distribution of $Y$?
		- $Y \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(n, \theta)$
			- $\alpha \to$ shape
			- $\theta \to$ scale
		- **N.B.**: how would you sample from exponential from rate $\theta$
			- then you can get the sample from gamma by summing up $n$ times
			- re: multi-carlo simulation
		- **N.B.**: If you sum two mgfs from poisson, would you still have a poisson?

- **Example.** Suppose $X_{1} \sim Pois(\lambda_{1}) \quad \lambda_{1} > 0$
	- $X_{2} \sim Pois(\lambda_{2}) \quad \lambda_{2} > 0$
	- $X_{1} \perp\!\!\perp X_{2}$
	- Set $Y = X_{1} + X_{2}$
		
	1. What is the MGF of $Y$?
		- same as $M_{Y}(t) = M_{X_{1} + X_{2}}(t) = \mathbb{E}\left( e^{t(X_{1} + X_{2})} \right) = \mathbb{E}\left( e^{tX_{1}} \cdot e^{tX_{2}} \right)$
		- $= \mathbb{E}\left( e^{tX_{1}} \right) \mathbb{E}\left( e^{tX_{2}} \right) = M_{X_{1}}(t) \cdot M_{X_{2}}(t)$
		- thus, the moment generation function of $X_{1}$ is...
			- $M_{X_{1}}(t) = e^{\lambda_{1} (e^t - 1)}$
			- $M_{X_{2}}(t) = e^{\lambda_{2}(e^{t}-1)}$
		- $\Rightarrow e^{(\lambda_{1} + \lambda_{2})(e^t -1)}$
			- we end up with another Poisson distribution
	2. What is the distribution of $Y$?
		- $Y \sim Pois(\lambda)$ where $\lambda = \lambda_{1} + \lambda_{2}$

- **Example.** Suppose $X_{1}\dots X_{n}$ are independent $\chi^{2}$ with $r_{1} \dots r_{n}$ degrees of freedom respectively.
	- What is the distribution of $Y = X_{1} + \cdots + X_{n}$?
	- **Answer.** $X_{i} \sim \chi_{r_{i}}^2 \longrightarrow X_{i} \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha = \dfrac{r_{i}}{2}; \theta = 2)$
		- $M_{X_{i}}(t) = \left( \dfrac{1}{1 - 2t} \right)^{\dfrac{r_{i}}{2}} = 1 = 1 \cdots n$
	- $M_{Y}(t) = M_{X_{1} + \cdots + X_{n}}(t)$
		- $= M_{X_{1}}(t) M_{X_{2}}(t) \cdots M_{X_{n}}(t)$
		- $= \left( \dfrac{1}{1 - 2t} \right)^{\dfrac{r_{1}}{2}} \cdot \left( \dfrac{1}{1-2t} \right)^{\dfrac{r_{2}}{2}} \cdot \cdots \left( \dfrac{1}{1-2t} \right)^{\dfrac{r_{n}}{2}}$
		- $= \left( \dfrac{1}{1-2t} \right)^{\dfrac{r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{n}}{2}}$
		- $Y \sim \chi_{(r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{n})}^2$ with degrees of freedom $df = r_{1} + r_{2} + \cdots + r_{n}$

- **Remark.**
	- If $Z \sim N(0, 1)$
	- Then $X = Z^2 \sim \chi_{(1)}^2$
		- if you sample from standard normal, then you already have $\chi^{2}$
	- Now, suppose $Z_{1}\dots Z_{n} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0,1)$
	- What is the distribution of $Z_{1}^2 + \cdots + Z_{n}^2 \; \boxed{ \sim \chi_{(n)}^2 }$
		- **N.B.**: another $\chi^{2}$ with $n$ degrees of freedom

## Distribution of $\mathbf{Y = g(x)}$

- **Theorem.** Suppose $X$ is a continuous random variable with support $\mathcal{S}_{X}$
	- Let $Y = g(x)$ be a differentiable function in $X$.
	- Then, the pdf of $Y$ is given by $f_{Y}(y) = f_{X}\left( g^{-1}(y) \right) \cdot \Bigg\lvert \dfrac{dx}{dy} \Bigg\rvert$ and the support of $Y$ is given by $\mathcal{S}_{Y} = \left\{ y: y = g(x),\ x \in S_{X} \right\}$
		- $\dfrac{dx}{dy}$ is what we call the Jacobian

- **Example.** Suppose $X$ has the pdf $f(x) = \begin{cases} 4x^3 & 0 < x < 1 \\ 0 & \mathrm{elsewhere} \end{cases}$
	- **N.B.**: the support of $X$ is $S_{X} = (0,1) \quad 0 < x < 1$
	- **N.B.**: first thing we should do is work the support of $Y$
	- Find the pdf of $Y = -2 \ln(X)$
		- $y = -2 \ln x$
		- $g(x) = -2\ln x$
		- recall: when $x$ is between $0$ and $1$, $\ln x$ is always negative
			- see graph of $\ln x$ to see why that's the case
		- so, we know $\ln x < 0$
			- $\Rightarrow -2 \ln x > 0$
		- $S_{Y} = (0, \infty)$
	- solve for $x$ to get $g^{-1}(y)$
		- $y = -2 \ln x$
		- $\frac{y}{-2} = \ln x$
		- $e^{-y/2} = x = g^{-1}(y)$
	- next find $\dfrac{dx}{dy}$
		- $\dfrac{dx}{dy} = e^{-y/2} \cdot \left( -\frac{1}{2} \right)$
		- $\Bigg\lvert  \dfrac{dx}{dy}  \Bigg\rvert = \frac{1}{2} e^{-y/2}$
	- now we're ready to write down the pdf
		- take $f_{Y}(y) = f_{X}(g^{-1}(y)) \Bigg\lvert  \dfrac{dx}{dy}  \Bigg\rvert$ and plug into $x$ of the pdf
		- so we have...
			- $f_{Y}(y) = 4 \cdot (e^{-y/2})^3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} e^{-y/2} \quad 0 < y < \infty$
			- $= 2 e^{-\frac{-3y}{2}} \cdot e^{-y/2} \quad 0 < y < \infty$
		- $\boxed{ f_{Y}(y) = \begin{cases} 2e^{-2y} & 0 < y < \infty \\ 0 & \mathrm{elsewhere} \end{cases} }$
